The Inference Engineering Book

Formula sheet

Every formula the book uses, every symbol defined, with the chapter that owns each derivation. Derivations live in the chapters; this page is the reference you keep open while capacity planning.

How to read the numbers

Numbers on this page are derived — arithmetic from these formulas applied to published model shapes and cited hardware specs. Nothing here was measured. The reference hardware for the whole book is H100 SXM: 989.4 TFLOP/s dense bf16, 3.35 TB/s HBM (NVIDIA published peaks), giving a roofline ridge of I* = 295 FLOP/byte. Lab 01 replaces those vendor peaks with measured values; every derived number moves with them.

§2

Memory and capacity

KV cache, one token, all layers — the "cell size"

$$k \;=\; c \;=\; 2 \cdot L \cdot h_{kv} \cdot d_h \cdot b$$

The leading 2 is K and V. It does not depend on h — only on KV heads, which is exactly why GQA and MQA shrink the cache. 128 KiB/token for Llama-3-8B bf16, 320 KiB/token for 70B. Owned by §2.1.

KV cache, full batch

$$\text{bytes}_{\text{KV}} = k \cdot \sum_{i=1}^{B} s_i$$

Linear in total resident tokens, not in batch size. Many short requests and few long ones cost the same. Owned by §2.1.

MLA cell size

$$c_{\text{MLA}} = L \cdot (r_{kv} + d_{\text{rope}}) \cdot b$$

One latent vector per token per layer instead of per-head K and V. Owned by §2.1; the mechanism is §7.2. Caveat: MLA caches are replicated across tensor-parallel ranks, so unlike GQA the cluster-wide footprint does not shrink as TP grows.

Group size, and the cell size written through it

$$g \equiv \frac{h}{h_{kv}}, \qquad c \;=\; 2 L h_{kv} d_h b \;=\; \frac{2\,L\,h\,d_h\,b}{g}$$

The same cell size, with the group size made explicit: every doubling of g halves the cache and doubles decode attention intensity, and one axis moves both. Owned by §3.5.

Cluster KV capacity, GQA versus MLA

$$N_{\text{GQA}} = \frac{P \cdot \min(\mathrm{TP},\, h_{kv})}{c}, \qquad N_{\text{MLA}} = \frac{P}{c_{\text{MLA}}}$$

P here is the KV pool per GPU in bytes and N the cluster-wide distinct resident tokens. GQA's capacity grows linearly in TP until TP reaches h_kv and then flattens — that saturation is the max(1, h_kv // tp_size) replication. MLA's is a constant, because its latent is replicated on every rank. The crossover is where min(TP, h_kv) passes c / c_MLA: 4.66 for Llama-3-70B against DeepSeek-V3, so MLA's cluster-KV advantage is spent by TP ≈ 5 (derived). Owned by §3.5; the same law with the weight term folded into P produces §5.1's tensor-parallel capacity table.

Block accounting

$$\text{blocks} = \lceil s / B_{\text{blk}} \rceil, \qquad \text{internal fragmentation} = B_{\text{blk}}\lceil s/B_{\text{blk}} \rceil - s \le B_{\text{blk}} - 1$$
$$\text{slot} = \text{block\_table}[r]\!\left[\lfloor p / B_{\text{blk}} \rfloor\right] \cdot B_{\text{blk}} + (p \bmod B_{\text{blk}})$$

The slot index is what the kernel is actually handed. Owned by §2.2.

Block reuse under alignment

$$\text{reused} = B_{\text{blk}}\lfloor m/B_{\text{blk}} \rfloor, \qquad \text{loss} = m \bmod B_{\text{blk}} \in [0,\, B_{\text{blk}}-1]$$

For m matching tokens. The loss is bounded by one block — about 15 tokens, or 0.68 ms of Llama-3-8B prefill. Small, and the book says so rather than overselling it. Owned by §2.3.

The VRAM budget

$$\text{bytes}_{\text{KV pool}} = C_{\text{total}} \cdot u - \text{bytes}_{\text{weights}} - \text{bytes}_{\text{act}} - \text{bytes}_{\text{overhead}}$$

u is gpu_memory_utilization — 0.92 by default in vLLM at this SHA, not the 0.90 usually quoted. vLLM measures the activation term by profiling; SGLang's mem_fraction_static is a heuristic reserve, not a measurement, so the two are not interchangeable. Owned by §2.6.

Maximum concurrency

$$B_{\max} = \left\lfloor \frac{\text{bytes}_{\text{KV pool}}}{k \cdot \bar{s}} \right\rfloor$$

The single most useful back-of-envelope in inference serving.

KV / weight traffic crossover

$$N^{*} = \frac{P\,b_w}{k}$$

The resident-token count at which KV traffic per decode step equals weight traffic — so the point where KV quantization becomes a latency lever rather than only a capacity one. 1.23 × 10⁵ tokens for Llama-3-8B bf16; 4.31 × 10⁵ for 70B. Below it, fp8 KV changes step bytes by under 1%. Owned by §2.5.

Hierarchical tiers

$$R^{*}_{\text{read}} = \frac{B_{\text{tier}}}{k}, \qquad T_{\text{tier}} \approx \frac{C_{\text{tier}}}{\lambda}$$

The prefill rate below which fetching from a tier beats recomputing, and how long a tier holds a prefix at arrival rate λ. Owned by §2.6.

§3

Performance and the roofline

Arithmetic intensity and the roofline

$$I = \frac{F}{Q}, \qquad \text{achievable FLOP/s} = \min(\pi,\; \beta I), \qquad I^{*} = \pi/\beta$$

Below the ridge you are memory-bound and only fewer bytes or more bandwidth helps; above it only more FLOP/s does. Owned by §0.4.

Prefill GEMM intensity, and where it crosses the ridge

$$I(T) = \frac{2Td}{b(2T+d)} \quad\text{(square)}, \qquad I(T) = \frac{2TKN}{b(TK+KN+TN)} \quad\text{(general)}$$
$$T^{*} = \frac{I^{*} d}{d - b\,I^{*}} \;=\; 345 \text{ tokens for a } 4096\times4096 \text{ bf16 GEMM on H100}$$

Intensity grows with the number of tokens in the pass — which is why batching is the single biggest throughput lever, and why a chunk smaller than I* cannot amortise its own weight stream. Owned by §0.4 and §1.1.

Decode intensities

$$I_{\text{GEMV}} \to \frac{2}{b} = 1.0, \qquad I_{\text{attn}} = \frac{2h}{b\,h_{kv}} = \frac{2g}{b} = 4 \text{ for Llama-3-8B}$$

Both are two orders of magnitude below the 295 ridge, and neither depends on sequence length or batch size. GQA raises attention intensity linearly in the group size. Owned by §0.4.

Decode step floor

$$t_{\text{decode}} \;\gtrsim\; \frac{\text{bytes}_{\text{weights}} + \text{bytes}_{\text{KV read}}}{\beta}$$

4.48 ms → 223 tok/s at batch 1 for Llama-3-8B bf16 on H100, of which 98% of the traffic is weights. This bound explains most of what quantization buys and why CUDA graphs only matter once you are close to it. Owned by §0.4.

Decode-step FLOPs, cached vs uncached

$$F_{\text{cached}} = 2P + 4\,L\,h\,d_h\,s, \qquad F_{\text{uncached}} = 2Ps + 2\,L\,h\,d_h\,s^{2}$$

At s = 2560 for Llama-3-8B, attention is only 7.0% of cached decode FLOPs (4.1% at 70B). The cache's payoff is deleting redundant weight GEMMs, not saving attention arithmetic — which is why decode attention kernels optimise a small share of FLOPs but a large share of latency. Owned by §0.2.

Prefill FLOPs

$$F_{\text{prefill}}(S) \approx 2PS + 2\,L\,h\,d_h\,S^{2}$$

Owned by §1.4, where it prices recompute; restated by §1.5 and §2.3.

Quantised-GEMM crossover batch size

$$M^{*} = \frac{b_{\text{eff}}}{8}\cdot\frac{P}{2\,\beta}$$

The batch at which a quantised weight GEMM stops being weight-bandwidth-bound and the dequantisation work starts to cost more than it saves. M* ≈ 76 (W4A16), 148 (W8A16 FP8), 295 (W8A8 FP8) on H100 SXM — which is why kernels dispatch on M. Owned by §4.3.

Effective bits per weight, and where a weight format stops being memory-bound

$$b_{\text{eff}} = b + \frac{b_s}{g} + \underbrace{\frac{b_z}{g}}_{\text{asymmetric only}}, \qquad B^{*} = \frac{I^{*}\,b_w}{2}$$

b stored bits per weight, b_s bits per scale, b_z bits per zero-point, g the group size. The second expression is the batch at which a weight format crosses the ridge, and it contains no model term at all: 295 for bf16, 148 for 8-bit weight-only, 74 for int4 group-128, and 295 again for W8A8 fp8 because I* doubles with the format. That is why the crossovers are worth memorising and the absolute latencies are not — tensor parallelism moves the latencies and leaves every boundary where it was. Owned by §4.1; the decision it feeds is §4.4.

Tensor-core row utilisation and launch overhead

$$U = \frac{\min(B, M_{\text{inst}})}{M_{\text{inst}}}, \qquad t_{\text{launch,total}} = N_{\text{kernels}} \cdot t_{\text{launch}}$$

A decode step with one row wastes most of an m16n8k16 tile. Introduced in §0.3; launch overhead is owned by §8.1.

Host budget per kernel, and whether you are launch-bound

$$B_{\text{host}} = \frac{T_{\text{GPU}}}{N_{\text{kernels}}}$$

A launch is asynchronous, so its cost only hurts when the CPU cannot stay ahead. Divide the device time of one step by the launches in it and compare against the host cost per launch — a cited 2.374 µs of driver time per cudaLaunchKernel on H100 with CUDA 12.6 (Vellaisamy et al., ISPASS 2025, Table V), with the PyTorch and Python layers on top of it and unquantified. For Llama-3-8B this gives 13.6 µs of budget at TP=1 against 0.78 ms of driver time for 330 launches (17%, marginal) and 1.42 µs at TP=8 against 0.94 ms for 395 launches — launch-bound before Python is considered (derived). Tensor parallelism shrinks T_GPU and grows N_kernels, and the two effects multiply, so the graph question gets sharper as you scale out. Owned by §8.1.

§4

Attention kernels

Materialised attention: traffic and intensity

$$Q = 4hT^{2}b + 2b\,d_h T (h + h_{kv}), \qquad I_{\text{materialised}} = \frac{T d_h}{b(T + d_h)}$$

Note what this says: the intensity of textbook attention is independent of the head count, so it is model-size-invariant — and it asymptotes to d_h/2 = 64, permanently below the 295 ridge. No amount of scaling rescues it. Owned by §3.1.

Compulsory attention intensity

$$I_{\text{compulsory}} = \frac{2hT}{b(h + h_{kv})}$$

FlashAttention intensity

$$I_{\text{flash}} = \frac{T}{2}$$

Grows without bound in T, crossing the ridge at T ≈ 590 tokens. The contrast with the materialised form above is the whole argument for FlashAttention in two lines. Owned by §3.2.

Online softmax recurrence

$$m^{(j)} = \max\!\left(m^{(j-1)},\, \max_{B_j} x\right), \qquad \alpha^{(j)} = e^{\,m^{(j-1)}-m^{(j)}}$$
$$\ell^{(j)} = \alpha^{(j)}\ell^{(j-1)} + \sum_{B_j} e^{\,x-m^{(j)}}, \qquad \tilde{O}^{(j)} = \alpha^{(j)}\tilde{O}^{(j-1)} + \sum_{B_j} e^{\,x-m^{(j)}} v$$
$$o = \tilde{O}^{(n)} / \ell^{(n)}$$

Exactly equal to standard softmax — proved by induction in §3.1, and verified numerically there on a worked 8-logit example. This recurrence is the foundation of FlashAttention and of every split-K decode kernel.

Log-sum-exp merge

$$O = \frac{e^{\mathrm{LSE}_A}O_A + e^{\mathrm{LSE}_B}O_B}{e^{\mathrm{LSE}_A}+e^{\mathrm{LSE}_B}}, \qquad \mathrm{LSE} = \log\!\left(e^{\mathrm{LSE}_A}+e^{\mathrm{LSE}_B}\right)$$

How two partial attention results over disjoint key ranges combine. Both engines pass a single fp32 LSE scalar per (token, head) between kernels to represent a partial softmax state — this is the interface that makes split-K possible. Owned by §3.1.

Overflow threshold

$$x_{\max} = \ln(\text{max finite value of the dtype}) \;=\; 11.09 \text{ (fp16)}, \quad 88.72 \text{ (bf16, fp32)}$$

Why the max subtraction is not optional.

FlashAttention tile constraints

$$2 d_h (B_r + 2 s B_c) \le \text{SMEM}, \qquad B_r(d_h + B_c) \lesssim 51{,}200 \text{ (256-thread block)}$$
$$\text{grid}_{\text{FA2}} = \text{batch} \times h \times \lceil T_q / B_r \rceil, \qquad \text{FLOPs}_{\text{rescale}} = \frac{T^{2} d_h}{B_c}$$

Shared memory and the register file jointly pin the tile shape; the FA2 grid shows why decode (T_q = 1) leaves the machine idle and needs §3.3's split-K. Owned by §3.2.

MLA decode intensity, and what tensor parallelism does to it

$$I_{\text{MLA}} = \frac{2h\,(2 r_{kv} + d_{\text{rope}})}{b\,(r_{kv} + d_{\text{rope}})} \;=\; 242 \text{ FLOP/byte for DeepSeek-V3 at TP=1}$$

All h query heads attend over one shared latent: each scores against r_kv + d_rope dimensions and outputs over r_kv, while the bytes read are one r_kv + d_rope-wide row per position. At 242 against the H100's 295 ridge, MLA decode attention is 82% of the way to compute-bound — a place no GQA model gets near, because MLA did not find a cheaper way to store attention state, it found a way to pay in FLOPs instead of bytes. The catch is the TP behaviour: query heads shard and the latent does not, so per-rank intensity falls as 1/TP — 242, 30, 15 at TP = 1, 8, 16 (derived), while GQA's I = 2g/b is TP-invariant because both head counts shard together. Owned by §3.5.

Split-K decode grid

$$G_{\text{2D}} = \left\lceil \frac{B}{\text{BLOCK\_Q}} \right\rceil h_{kv}, \qquad G_{\text{3D}} = G_{\text{2D}}\cdot S, \qquad S^{*} = \left\lceil \frac{N_{SM}}{G_{\text{2D}}} \right\rceil$$

S is the number of KV splits and N_SM = 132 on H100 SXM. S* is the smallest split count that first fills the machine; splitting past it buys nothing and costs merge traffic, so every implementation clamps. Llama-3-8B at batch 1 gives G_2D = 8 and S* = 17; Llama-3-70B at TP=8 batch 1 gives G_2D = 1 and S* = 132, against vLLM's fixed 16 — which is exactly the axis on which SGLang's adaptive policy differs. Owned by §3.3; the same batch-dependence, seen as a bug rather than a feature, is §10.4.

Number formats

$$\text{max\_normal} = (2 - 2^{-m}) \cdot 2^{(2^{e}-2)-B}, \quad B = 2^{e-1}-1, \qquad b_{\text{eff}} = b + \frac{s_{\text{bits}}}{g_{\text{blk}}}$$

Effective bits per weight including group scales: 4.25 for MXFP4 (block 32, E8M0 scale), 4.5 for NVFP4 (block 16, E4M3), 4.125 for int4 with 128-element groups. Owned by §0.5.

§5

Scheduling

Static batch utilisation

$$U_{\text{static}} = \frac{\sum_i n_i}{B \max_i n_i} = \frac{\bar{n}}{n_{\max}}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[U_{\text{static}}] \approx \frac{1}{H_B} \approx \frac{1}{\ln B + \gamma}$$

For exponentially distributed output lengths. At B = 32 that is roughly a quarter of the machine spent on finished rows — the number that motivated continuous batching. Owned by §1.3.

Chunked prefill traffic and chunk sizing

$$\text{bytes}_{\text{HBM}}(c) = \frac{S}{c}\cdot 2P b_w + k c \frac{N(N-1)}{2} \approx \frac{S}{c}\left(2P b_w + \frac{kS}{2}\right)$$
$$c \approx \frac{\pi_{\text{eff}}(\text{ITL}_{\text{target}} - t_{\text{decode}})}{2P}, \qquad c \gtrsim I^{*}$$

Both terms scale as 1/c, but the weight re-streaming term dominates: the KV re-read is only 13% of it at 32k on Llama-3-8B and would not overtake until ~245k tokens. And chunking ramps ITL rather than flattening it — later chunks attend to more keys. Owned by §1.5.

Recompute vs swap crossover

$$R^{*} = \frac{B_{\text{pcie}}}{k}, \qquad f^{*} = 1 - \frac{k R}{B_{\text{pcie}}}$$

Where f is the prefix-cache survival fraction of a preemption victim. Largely historical for vLLM, which dropped swapping entirely; still live for SGLang's decode-role disaggregation. Owned by §1.4.

Prefix-cache time saved

$$T_{\text{saved}} = \frac{\sum_i \text{matched\_tokens}_i}{R_{\text{prefill}}}$$

Owned by §2.4. Note the reported hit length is capped at num_tokens − 1, so a fully cached prompt can never report 100%.

Disaggregation viability

$$\frac{t_{\text{xfer}}}{t_{\text{prefill}}} = \frac{k}{\mathrm{BW}} \cdot \frac{\mathrm{MFU}\cdot F}{2N}$$

Prompt length cancels. Whether prefill–decode disaggregation is viable is a property of model shape and interconnect, not of your workload — which is why it can be decided at design time. Owned by §1.6.

§6

Latency, queueing, and cost

Metric definitions

$$\text{TTFT} = t_1 - t_a, \qquad \text{ITL}_k = t_k - t_{k-1}, \qquad \text{TPOT} = \frac{t_N - t_1}{N-1}, \qquad \text{E2EL} = t_N - t_a$$

Owned by §1.2. The two benchmark harnesses do not implement these identically — vLLM samples ITL per streamed chunk, SGLang per token — so ITL and TPOT are not comparable across them.

Queueing

$$W_{p99} = \frac{S\ln(100\rho)}{1-\rho}, \qquad \text{TTFT}_{p99} \approx S + W_{p99}, \qquad \bar{L} = \lambda \bar{W}$$
$$\mathbb{E}[W] \approx \frac{\rho}{1-\rho} \cdot \frac{C_a^{2}+C_s^{2}}{2} \cdot S \quad \text{(Kingman, general arrivals)}$$

At high utilisation, TTFT p99 is 90–99% queueing and only a few percent prefill — so tuning the model to shave prefill is the wrong lever. Owned by §1.2; Little's Law is reused in §10.5.

Cost per million output tokens

$$\text{cost}_{\text{1M}} = \frac{10^{6} \cdot \text{price}_{\text{GPU-hour}} \cdot N_{\text{GPU}}}{3600 \cdot \text{goodput}_{\text{tok/s}}}$$

Goodput, not throughput: tokens delivered outside the SLO are tokens you cannot sell. Owned by §10.5.

§7

Speculative decoding

Expected accepted length

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{tokens per step}] = \frac{1 - \alpha^{\,\kappa+1}}{1 - \alpha}$$

For a chain draft of length κ with i.i.d. per-token acceptance α. The bonus token from the target model is why the exponent is κ+1.

Batch-aware speedup, and where speculation stops paying

$$S(B) = \frac{E\max\!\left(1,\tfrac{B}{T^{*}}\right)}{\max\!\left(1,\tfrac{B(\kappa+1)}{T^{*}}\right) + \kappa c\max\!\left(1,\tfrac{B}{T^{*}}\right)}, \qquad S_{\infty} = \frac{E}{\kappa+1+\kappa c} < 1$$
$$B^{\star} = \frac{T^{*}(E - \kappa c)}{\kappa + 1}$$

The compute-bound asymptote is below 1 unconditionally — past enough batch, speculation always loses. B* ≈ 150 concurrent sequences for Llama-3-8B drafted by Llama-3.2-1B on H100 at κ=3, α=0.70. Owned by §6.2.

Speedup condition

$$\text{speedup} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[\text{tokens per step}]}{1 + \kappa c}$$

With c the cost of one draft step relative to one target step — derivable as the streamed-weight-byte ratio while both are memory-bound. The batch-aware form above says where the loss begins. Owned by §6.2.

Acceptance rate as a distance

$$\alpha = \sum_x \min\big(p(x),\,q(x)\big) = 1 - D_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,\,q)$$

Acceptance is exactly one minus the total-variation distance between target and draft. The proof of distribution-preservation turns on the residual identity $\sum_x \max(0, p-q) = 1 - \sum_x \min(p, q)$. Owned by §6.2.

Draft cost as a parameter ratio, and the indifference curve

$$c \;=\; \frac{t_D}{t_T} \;=\; \frac{N_D^{\text{stream}}}{N_T^{\text{stream}}}, \qquad c(\alpha) \;=\; \frac{1}{\kappa}\left(\frac{E(\alpha,\kappa)}{s} - 1\right)$$

Both target and draft are memory-bound at decode, so time is bytes over bandwidth and the bandwidth cancels: c is exactly the ratio of parameters actually streamed — every matmul weight plus the output head, but not the gathered input embedding table. Under equal tensor parallelism the shards divide out too. A Llama-3.2-1B draft costs 16.5% of a Llama-3-8B step and 1.8% of a Llama-3-70B one (derived) — a ninefold change from one target swap with the draft untouched. The second expression is the indifference curve: the locus of (α, c) pairs delivering the same speedup s. Where it crosses c = 0 is the acceptance a zero-cost matcher needs to tie a draft model — 0.43 against the 8B target, 0.67 against the 70B one, and moving less than 0.05 as κ goes from 1 to 7. The threshold is almost entirely a function of c and barely of κ. Owned by §6.3.

Tree verification

$$E_{\text{tree}} \;=\; \sum_{i=0}^{D}\ \prod_{j=1}^{i}\beta_j, \qquad \text{speedup} \;=\; \frac{E_{\text{tree}}}{1 + S c} \quad\text{while } T\cdot B \;<\; I^{*}$$

T verified nodes, D maximum tree depth, S draft forward passes per iteration, and β_i the probability that the target's continuation at depth i appears among the tree's candidates there, given depth i−1 was accepted. Three checks. Setting topk = 1 collapses it to §6.2's chain exactly. T is absent from the denominator — that is the whole reason trees work, because below the ridge the verify pass still costs the bandwidth floor. And β_i ≥ α strictly whenever topk > 1, since k candidates get k chances at one argmax; the gain saturates fast, which is why global reranking across depths beats a larger branching factor. Above the ridge, multiply the verify term by TB/I* and §6.2's S(B) takes over — the break-even batch shrinks by roughly T/(κ+1). Owned by §6.4.

Parallel drafting

$$\text{step cost} \;=\; 1 + c \quad\text{(not } 1 + \kappa c\text{)}, \qquad \alpha^{*}\ \text{solves}\ E(\alpha,\kappa) = 1 + c$$

Emitting all κ draft tokens in one forward pass collapses the cost term. At κ = 3, c = 0.165 that is a 28% larger speedup at unchanged α, and it drops the break-even acceptance from 0.34 to 0.14 (derived) — a parallel drafter can be less than half as accurate as a chain drafter and still win at batch 1. It does not rescue the compute-bound regime: substituting κc → c into §6.2's asymptote still leaves S_∞ = E/((κ+1) + c) < 1. Owned by §6.6.

§8

Parallelism and collectives

Ring all-reduce volume, and one transformer forward pass

$$V_{\text{AR}}(S, p) \;=\; 2\,\frac{p-1}{p}\,S, \qquad V_{\text{step}} \;=\; 2L \cdot 2\,\frac{p-1}{p}\, n\,d\,b$$

S bytes per collective, p ranks, n tokens in the pass. The 2(p−1)/p coefficient is provably the minimum any rank must send, and it tends to 2S rather than pS — which is why ring is the default. The 2L is the two all-reduces per block. For Llama-3-70B at TP=8 one token costs 4.375 MiB per rank per token (derived). Owned by §5.1.

The comm fraction, and where TP stops paying

$$\frac{t_{\text{comm}}}{t_{\text{floor}}} \;=\; \frac{4L\,n\,d\,b\,\beta}{2P\,B_{\text{net}}}\,(p-1), \qquad I_{\text{net}} \;=\; \frac{P}{2L\,(p-1)\,d\,b}$$

B_net is interconnect bandwidth, β HBM bandwidth, P the parameter count. The first expression is linear in batch and in p−1, and for Llama-3-70B on H100 + NVLink it is 1.39 × 10⁻⁴ · n · (p−1): negligible at batch 1, 25% of the step floor at batch 256 and TP=8, over 100% at batch 256 and TP=32 (derived). The second is an interconnect arithmetic intensity — FLOPs of compute per byte of TP traffic — and comparing it against the machine's own π/B_net puts break-even at p ≈ 25 on NVLink and p ≈ 2.35 across InfiniBand NDR. Tensor parallelism is a single-NVLink-domain technique, and the arithmetic says so by a factor of eighteen. Note also what neither expression contains: at small batch the binding constraint is not bandwidth but the 2L fixed collective latencies, which TP does not amortise. Owned by §5.1.

Pipeline bubble, and why PP buys no latency

$$T_{\text{total}} = (M + S - 1)\,t, \qquad \beta_{\text{bubble}} = \frac{S-1}{S+M-1}$$
$$T_{\text{token}} = S\,t = \frac{W}{B_{\text{HBM}}}, \qquad \text{throughput} = \frac{S\,B_{\text{HBM}}}{W}$$

S stages, M micro-batches, t one stage on one micro-batch, W the weight bytes one token forces off HBM. S cancels out of the latency. A token still traverses every layer and still forces the same W bytes, whether those bytes sit on one GPU or sixteen — so any argument that pipeline parallelism reduces per-token latency is wrong. What S buys is throughput, linearly, and the ability to host S× the parameters. And GPipe's remedy inverts at decode: t is set by weight traffic, which does not shrink when the micro-batch does, so splitting a decode batch into M pieces multiplies makespan by (M+S−1)/M for nothing. Owned by §5.2.

DP-attention boundary, and the MoE all-to-all

$$V_{\text{dp}} = 2\,\frac{N-1}{N}\, T_g \, d \, b, \qquad V_{\text{dispatch}} = T_\ell \cdot \rho \cdot d \cdot b, \qquad \rho = N\left(1 - \left(1 - \tfrac{1}{N}\right)^{k}\right)$$

N the world size, T_g the global token count this step, T_ℓ the rank's local token count, k the experts per token. ρ is the expected number of distinct destination ranks per token — real dispatch libraries deduplicate, so two chosen experts on one rank cost one crossing. As EP widens, deduplication stops helping: ρ goes 5.25, 6.45, 7.18, 7.62 at EP = 8, 16, 32, 64 for top-8 routing. The counter-intuitive result is that the all-to-all is cheaper in bytes than the alternative — 14.6 MB per layer per rank against 25.7 MB for gather-plus-TP-FFN at EP=8 (derived). What makes it hard is that per-peer sizes are data-dependent, there are two serialised round trips per layer, and bandwidth drops an order of magnitude outside the NVLink domain. Owned by §5.3.

Collective cost models, and the algorithm crossover

$$T_{\text{ring}} = \alpha + 2(N-1)\,\alpha_{\text{step}} + \frac{2(N-1)}{N}\frac{S}{B}, \qquad T_{\text{1shot}} = \alpha + \frac{(N-1)S}{B}, \qquad T_{\text{2shot}} = \alpha + \alpha_{\text{step}} + \frac{2(N-1)S}{NB}$$
$$S^{*} = \frac{N\,B\,\alpha_{\text{step}}}{(N-1)(N-2)}$$

α is the fixed per-invocation cost, α_step the incremental cost of one more lock-step synchronisation, B the achievable unidirectional link bandwidth. Ring achieves the minimum wire bytes and pays 2(N−1) rounds for them; two-shot achieves the same byte count in two rounds because a full mesh lets a rank address every peer at once; one-shot spends (N−1)S bytes to get down to one round. Running vLLM's two hard-coded constants backwards through S* yields an implied barrier cost of 1.75 µs at N = 4 and 3.06 µs at N = 8 — the same microsecond band §0.3 gives for a device-wide sync, growing with N as an N-way rendezvous should. Note S* → ∞ as N → 2, which is why two-shot is never used at world size 2. Owned by §5.4.

Collective barriers per second

$$N_{\text{barrier}} = 2L \text{ per step}, \qquad \text{barriers/s} = 2L \big/ t_{\text{step}}$$

Llama-3-70B at TP=8 crosses 160 collective barriers per rank per step against a 5.25 ms floor — roughly 30,000 barriers per second per rank at the roofline (derived). Every one is a chance for two ranks to disagree, and a disagreement is not a bad token but the end of the server. That asymmetry is what justifies paying for a plan broadcast. Owned by §5.5.

§9

Architectures: MoE, MLA, SSM, LoRA

How many experts a batch touches

$$E_{\text{touched}}(B) \;=\; E\left(1 - \left(1 - \frac{k}{E}\right)^{B}\right), \qquad B_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(1/2)}{\ln(1 - k/E)}$$

E routed experts, k experts per token, B tokens in the step. The same coupon-collector shape as ρ in §5.3, applied to experts instead of ranks, and its two limits are the story: E_touched(1) = k, and it reaches E fast. Half the layer by batch 22 for DeepSeek-V3 and 95% by batch 94 (derived) — so every production batch is in the stream-everything regime and sparsity buys FLOPs, not bytes. Owned by §7.1.

MoE arithmetic intensity and its ridge batch

$$I_{\text{MoE}}(B) \;=\; \frac{2\,B\,k}{b_w\, E_{\text{touched}}(B)} \;\longrightarrow\; I_{\text{dense}}(B)\cdot\frac{k}{E}, \qquad B^{*}_{\text{MoE}} \;=\; \frac{b_w\,I^{*}\,E}{2\,k}$$

d and d_ff cancel completely, so this is a property of E, k and the weight dtype and nothing else. At B = 1 it equals the dense GEMV intensity 2/b_w — at batch 1 an MoE layer and a dense layer are the same kind of object. Asymptotically the slope is the dense slope divided by E/k: 32 for DeepSeek-V3, 16 for Qwen3-30B-A3B. Hence batch 9,456 to reach an H100's fp8 ridge against 296 for a dense layer (derived), which is why wide expert parallelism is the shape of every serious frontier MoE deployment. Owned by §7.1; the same M* · E/k from the kernel side is §4.4's.

Grouped-GEMM padding factor

$$P(B) \;=\; \frac{\sum_e \lceil m_e / M_{\text{blk}} \rceil M_{\text{blk}}}{B\,k} \;\approx\; \frac{E_{\text{touched}}(B)\; M_{\text{blk}}}{B\,k}$$

m_e the rows routed to expert e, M_blk the tile height. When the mean row count M_e = Bk/E is below the tile height, every touched expert costs a full tile — 2–16× at decode-like batches. It is a tensor-core tax, not a bandwidth one: the padded rows are zeros that still occupy MMA slots. vLLM's autotuned configs independently rediscover M_e, pinning the tile at 2 M_e from batch 256 upward. In the masked layout the accounting is different and worse: total work is E · max_e m_e, not Σ_e m_e. Owned by §7.1.

MLA: absorbed versus naive, per layer per query token

$$\underbrace{2\,h\,r_{kv}(d_{\text{nope}}+d_v)}_{\text{q-fold} \;+\; \text{v-fold}} \;=\; \underbrace{2\,r_{kv}\,h\,(d_{\text{nope}}+d_v)}_{\text{the up-projection they replace}}$$
$$F_{\text{core}}^{\text{naive}} = 2h(d_{\text{nope}}+d_{\text{rope}}) + 2h\,d_v, \qquad F_{\text{core}}^{\text{absorbed}} = 2h(r_{kv}+d_{\text{rope}}) + 2h\,r_{kv}$$

The first identity is the whole trick in one line: the two folds cost exactly what the up-projection they replace costs, so absorption moves a fixed cost from the context axis to the query axis rather than deleting work. The second is the price: per-head key width goes 192 → 576 and value width 128 → 512, a flat 3.4× more FLOPs per context token for DeepSeek-V3 — which is what buys the 71× reduction in bytes touched. At decode there is no regime where decompressing wins (S* ≈ 1.006); at prefill the folds are paid Q times while the up-projection is paid once and shared, so absorption cannot win at any context once Q > 171, and a cold prefill with Q = S loses by exactly the 3.4×. Owned by §7.2. Note: vLLM's analytic FLOP model charges the naive widths on the decode path and therefore under-counts MLA decode attention by that same 3.4× — see §10.5.

Recurrent state, and where it beats a KV cache

$$\text{state} = \underbrace{d_{\text{conv}}\,(W-1)\,b}_{\text{short-conv}} + \underbrace{H_m\,d_h^m\,d_{\text{state}}\,b'}_{\text{SSM}} \text{ per layer}, \qquad s^{*} = \frac{\text{state}_{\text{total}}}{k}$$

d_conv = d_inner + 2 g d_state, W the depthwise kernel width, H_m SSM heads, b' the SSM dtype width (fp32 by default). Constant in context, but not small: 97.4 MiB per Nemotron-H-8B sequence, of which the conv state is 1.4%. The crossover s* against a KV cache of cell size k is 779 tokens against Llama-3-8B, or 395 with a bf16 SSM dtype (derived). Past a few hundred tokens the fixed state wins and the margin compounds — 16 GiB against 97.4 MiB at 128k. Owned by §7.3.

LoRA: adapter size and the decode multiplier

$$P(r) = r \sum_{\text{targets}} (d_{\text{in}} + d_{\text{out}}) \cdot L, \qquad \rho = \frac{P(r)}{N_{\text{base}}}, \qquad t_{\text{decode}} \propto 1 + k\,\rho$$

r the adapter rank, k the number of distinct active adapters in the batch. For Llama-3-8B with all seven linear targets, P(r) = r · 2,621,440 parameters — exactly r · 5 MiB in bf16 — so ρ = r · 3.264 × 10⁻⁴. The split by regime is the thing to internalise: prefill costs +ρ per token regardless of how many adapters are active, because it is compute-bound; decode costs k · ρ, because the base weights are read once but every distinct active adapter adds its own weight traffic. The rule: k · r ≲ 300 keeps it under 10% of a Llama-3-8B decode step. Larger base models tolerate more adapters at the same rank, which is slightly counter-intuitive. Owned by §7.5.

Image tokens, and the two multimodal ratios

$$N_{\text{img}} = \frac{H' W'}{(p\,m)^{2}}, \qquad I_{\text{enc}} \approx N_p, \qquad \frac{\text{KV bytes}}{\text{embedding bytes}} = \frac{2 L h_{kv} d_h}{d}$$

H', W' the resized dimensions, p the ViT patch size and m the spatial merge factor — 14 and 2 for Qwen2-VL, so one merged vision token is exactly 28² = 784 pixels and the processor's pixel cap is a token cap. A 1024×1024 screenshot is 1,369 LM tokens; an un-downscaled 4K one is 10,549 (derived). The encoder's intensity is roughly its patch count, so at 5,476 patches it is 18.6× past the H100 ridge — compute-bound at batch one, while decode is memory-bound at batch 256, in the same engine. The last ratio is why encode–prefill disaggregation ships 16× fewer bytes than P/D for Llama-3-8B shapes. Owned by §7.4.

§10

The serving system and routing

Frontend budget, and what a process split buys

$$c \;\le\; \frac{T_{\text{step}}}{B}, \qquad T_{\text{one process}} = T_{\text{step}} + Bc, \qquad T_{\text{two processes}} = \max\!\left(T_{\text{step}},\; Bc\right)$$

c is the host cost of turning one generated token into bytes on a socket — detokenise, build the delta, serialise, write — and B the batch. One forward pass yields one token per sequence and every sequence has its own socket, so the frontend does B units of c per step. The addition in the single-process form is not a modelling choice, it is CPython: one interpreter lock means the two loops cannot both execute bytecode. 140 µs of budget at batch 32, 35 µs at batch 128 against the 4.48 ms floor (derived) — and that budget is also exactly the c at which the split is worth 2×. Every serving system becomes frontend-bound at a batch size, not at a token rate. Owned by §9.3 and §11.2.

Cache dilution: what the load balancer does to your hit rate

$$H_{\text{rr}} = \min\!\left(1, \frac{M}{W}\right), \qquad H_{\text{aff}} = \min\!\left(1, \frac{MN}{W}\right), \qquad G_{\text{waste}} = \lambda\,(1 - H)\,t_{\text{prefill}}(L_p)$$

N replicas, M distinct cached prefixes one replica's pool holds, W the workload's distinct-prefix working set, λ the arrival rate, L_p the shared prefix length. Because caches are per-replica, round-robin replicates every hot prefix onto all N replicas, so the fleet's distinct-prefix capacity is one replica's — the dual of false sharing in a multi-core cache. For 1,000 tenant prompts of 2,048 tokens across 8 H100s that is 10.4% against 83.2%, i.e. 3.8 H100s of pure recompute (derived). Uniform popularity is the pessimistic case; cache-aware routing pays in proportion to how flat and wide your prefix distribution is. Owned by §9.4.

Grammar bitmask storage

$$\text{bytes/row} = \left\lceil \frac{|V|}{32} \right\rceil \times 4, \qquad \text{full table} = |Q| \times \text{bytes/row}$$

16,032 bytes per request per step at |V| = 128,256, and |Q| × 16,032 to materialise the whole state→mask table — 1.6 GB at 100k states, which is why no backend precomputes it and rows are computed lazily instead. Under speculative decoding the per-step buffer grows to max_num_seqs × (1 + κ) rows. Owned by §6.5.

§11

Benchmarking, warmup, and evidence

The prefix-cache ceiling

$$\phi = \frac{T_p}{T_p + T_d}, \qquad \text{speedup} \le \frac{1}{1 - \phi\,\eta}$$

T_p the GPU time a request's prefill costs and T_d its decode, both amortised to a per-request share of a batched step; φ the prefill fraction; η ∈ [0,1] the fraction of prefill tokens a perfect prefix cache would serve from an existing hit. This is Amdahl's law with prefill as the accelerable part, and it bounds the mechanism — not either engine.

Its use is to settle an argument before you have it. Worked for Llama-3-8B on an H100: chat (512 in / 1024 out) gives φ = 0.065 and a ceiling of 1.06×; RAG (2048/256) gives φ = 0.440 and 1.66×; an agent loop (8192/128, batch capped at 48 by KV fit) gives φ = 0.702 and 2.71×. So on chat traffic, a perfect prefix cache cannot buy more than six percent, and any benchmark claiming more is measuring something else. All derived arithmetic on published shapes; nothing measured. Owned by §13.2.

Rebase debt

$$R = r \cdot T$$

r upstream commits per week touching the files you must change, T the length of your project in weeks. R is an upper bound on conflicts rather than a count of them, and it is the quantity that decides where a project sits on the ladder. Counted from git log over the 29 weeks ending at each pinned SHA, vllm/v1/core/sched/scheduler.py took 115 commits while vllm/v1/core/sched/interface.py took 7 — both reachable through the same scheduler_cls seam, so extending the abstract base rather than the concrete class is a 16× reduction in what you have to keep up with for the same feature. Repository metadata, reproducible with the command named in the chapter; not a measurement. Owned by §13.4.

The two-constant workload model

$$t_{\text{pre}}(S) = c_p S + c_q S^{2}, \qquad t_{\text{dec}} = S_{\text{out}}\,\bar{s}\,c_d, \qquad \frac{t_{\text{pre}}}{t_{\text{dec}}} \approx \frac{c_p}{c_d}\cdot\frac{S_{\text{in}}}{S_{\text{out}}\,\bar{s}}$$

S_in, S_out the input and output lengths, s̄ the mean resident sequence length. Calibrated against this book's constants, Llama-3-8B on an H100 gives c_p = 40.6 µs, c_q = 6.63 × 10⁻¹⁰ s, c_d = 4.96 × 10⁻⁸ s and therefore c_p/c_d = 820. Two readings. Decode engine time is bilinear in output length and resident length, so doubling the input length doubles the cost of every decode step the request will ever take — that is how input length controls decode performance, and it is invisible if you look only at prefill. And the split swings from 82% prefill on summarisation to 2% on agentic traffic, with a balance point at x* = 551 tokens for a symmetric workload — the coincidence that makes "chat" feel neutral. Owned by §10.1.

Arrival variability is a flag

$$C_a^{2} \;=\; \frac{1}{\texttt{burstiness}}$$

vLLM draws inter-arrival gaps from a Gamma distribution whose shape parameter is --burstiness; mean 1/λ, variance 1/(λ²κ), so the flag is exactly the reciprocal of Kingman's arrival-variability term. SGLang samples exponential only and is pinned at C_a² = 1. Two engineers benchmarking "at 10 req/s" can therefore differ by 2× in mean queueing delay and 0% in throughput. Owned by §10.1.

Warmup is a queueing problem

$$t_{\mathrm{rel}} = \frac{S}{\left(1 - \sqrt{\rho}\right)^{2}}, \qquad N_{\mathrm{rel}} = \lambda\,t_{\mathrm{rel}} = \frac{\rho}{\left(1-\sqrt{\rho}\right)^{2}}$$

How long a queue started empty takes to approach its stationary distribution, and how many requests arrive in one time constant. Steady state wants roughly three. At ρ = 0.9 on the worked Llama-3-8B configuration that is 1,025 requests and 46 seconds (derived) — and it gets worse the closer you push to the part of the curve worth characterising, which is precisely why short benchmarks systematically understate tail latency. Both harnesses default to 0 or 1 warmup requests. Owned by §10.3.

An overloaded run measures your patience

$$W(t) = \frac{(\lambda-\mu)\,t}{\mu}, \qquad \bar{W} = \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(\tfrac{\lambda}{\mu}-1\right) T, \qquad W_{p99} \approx 0.198\,T \;\text{ at }\lambda = 1.2\mu$$

Past saturation the queue is not in steady state and never will be, so the backlog after T seconds is (λ−μ)T and the reported percentile is a linear function of run duration. 12 s, 59 s and 119 s of p99 wait for 60-, 300- and 600-second runs of the same server at the same offered load (derived). An overload number is uninterpretable without T, and two published ones are incomparable unless both ran for the same wall time. Owned by §10.3.

What a difference has to be before you may claim it

$$\mathrm{MDE} = (z_{0.975} + z_{0.80})\,\sigma\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n}} = \frac{3.96\,\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}, \qquad n = \left(\frac{3.96\,\mathrm{CV}}{\mathrm{MDE}}\right)^{2}$$

The smallest difference n runs per arm can defend at α = 0.05 with 80% power. There is no row for n = 1, because with one run you have no estimate of σ and therefore no claim. At a 5% coefficient of variation — what you get when the prefix cache is not flushed between runs or the GPU is thermally drifting — three runs cannot resolve anything smaller than 11%, which is larger than most published deltas. The fix is a lower CV, not more runs. Percentiles need their own sample count: do not quote a p99 from fewer than ~1,000 completed requests. Owned by §10.3.
